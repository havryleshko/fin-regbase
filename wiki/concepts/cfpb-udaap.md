# CFPB — UDAAP: Unfair, Deceptive, and Abusive Acts or Practices

## Overview

UDAAP is the CFPB's broadest and most frequently applied compliance standard. Section 1036 of the Dodd-Frank Act (12 USC 5536) prohibits any covered person or service provider from engaging in any unfair, deceptive, or abusive act or practice in connection with any consumer financial product or service.

UDAAP is not a checklist. It is a principles-based catch-all that applies to any consumer-facing conduct not already prohibited by an enumerated consumer law — and often in conjunction with enumerated law violations. The CFPB assesses UDAAP risk in every examination of every product line, regardless of whether specific violations of enumerated laws have been found.

UDAAP for consumer financial products draws on FTC Act §5 (the longstanding federal UDAP standard for deception and unfairness) and adds a third prong — "abusive" — which is unique to Dodd-Frank and the CFPB's jurisdiction. **(Dodd-Frank §1036; FTC Act §5)**

## The Three UDAAP Standards

### Deceptive Acts or Practices

A representation, omission, act, or practice is **deceptive** when all three of the following conditions are met:

1. **Misleads or is likely to mislead** — The representation, omission, act, or practice misleads or is likely to mislead the consumer.

2. **Reasonable consumer interpretation** — The consumer's interpretation of the representation, omission, act, or practice is reasonable under the circumstances.

3. **Material** — The misleading representation, omission, act, or practice is material — i.e., it is likely to affect the consumer's decision to purchase, use, or avoid the product or service.

**(CFPB Supervision and Examination Manual — Auto Finance §UDAAP; Dodd-Frank §1036)**

**Key characteristics of the deception standard:**
- Deception does not require intent. An honest mistake that misleads consumers is still deceptive if the other conditions are met.
- Omissions are as actionable as affirmative misrepresentations. Failing to disclose a material term can be deceptive.
- The "reasonable consumer" standard is objective — it asks whether a consumer acting reasonably would be misled, not whether the particular consumer actually was.
- "Likely to mislead" is a lower threshold than "does mislead" — the CFPB does not need to show actual consumer harm, only the realistic likelihood of harm.

**Common deception findings in practice:**
- Marketing that highlights benefits while obscuring fees, conditions, or limitations
- Use of terms such as "free," "rewards," or "discounts" without disclosing material restrictions
- Advertised terms that are not actually available to the consumer
- Inconsistency between marketing representations and contract terms
- Add-on products presented as mandatory when optional

### Unfair Acts or Practices

An act or practice is **unfair** when all three of the following conditions are met:

1. **Substantial injury** — It causes or is likely to cause substantial injury to consumers.

2. **Not reasonably avoidable** — The injury is not reasonably avoidable by consumers themselves.

3. **Not outweighed by countervailing benefits** — The injury is not outweighed by countervailing benefits to consumers or to competition.

**(CFPB Supervision and Examination Manual — Auto Finance §UDAAP; Dodd-Frank §1036)**

**Key characteristics of the unfairness standard:**
- "Substantial injury" includes financial harm (fees, charges, credit damage) and, in some contexts, non-financial harm (significant inconvenience, privacy harm). Small per-consumer harms can still be substantial in aggregate across a large consumer population.
- "Not reasonably avoidable" means the consumer could not reasonably have protected themselves — because of information asymmetry, complexity, or because the entity's conduct prevented avoidance. If the entity disclosed the harm clearly in advance and the consumer proceeded, avoidability is harder to establish.
- The "countervailing benefits" assessment is a net harm test. A practice that causes some consumer harm but produces significant benefits to competition or consumer welfare may not be unfair on balance.
- Unlike deception, unfairness can arise from deliberate exploitation of consumer decision-making limitations, not just from misrepresentation.

**Common unfairness findings in practice:**
- Charging fees that consumers cannot identify or anticipate from the disclosed terms
- Blocking consumers from accessing their funds or exercising account rights
- Processing payments in an order (e.g., largest to smallest) that maximises overdraft fees without disclosure
- Enrolling consumers in recurring charges without clear, advance, affirmative consent
- Add-on products or insurance enrolled at the point of sale without explicit consumer authorisation

### Abusive Acts or Practices

An act or practice is **abusive** when it either:

**Prong 1:** Materially interferes with the ability of a consumer to understand a term or condition of a consumer financial product or service; or

**Prong 2:** Takes unreasonable advantage of any of the following consumer vulnerabilities:
- **(a)** A **lack of understanding** on the part of the consumer of the material risks, costs, or conditions of the product or service;
- **(b)** The **inability of the consumer to protect their interests** in selecting or using the product or service; or
- **(c)** The **reasonable reliance** by the consumer on a covered person to act in the interests of the consumer.

**(Dodd-Frank §1036(a)(1)(B); 12 USC 5536(a)(1)(B))**

**Key characteristics of the abusiveness standard:**
- "Abusive" is unique to the CFPB — the FTC does not have an "abusive" prong. It was added by Dodd-Frank specifically to address conduct that exploits the structural power imbalance between financial firms and consumers.
- Prong 1 (material interference) addresses product complexity, disclosure obfuscation, and any conduct that makes it harder for the consumer to understand what they are buying. This overlaps with deception but is broader — interference with understanding does not require that the consumer is actually misled.
- Prong 2(c) — reasonable reliance on a covered person to act in the consumer's interests — is particularly significant for advisory, fiduciary-adjacent, or relationship-based financial products. A consumer who reasonably trusts their bank, lender, or financial service provider is protected against that provider exploiting the trust relationship.
- An act or practice can be simultaneously deceptive, unfair, and abusive. The CFPB may cite all three prongs from a single practice.
- The abusiveness standard does not require the consumer to suffer financial harm — interference with understanding is a standalone basis for a finding.

**Common abusiveness findings in practice:**
- Structuring product terms in ways designed to obscure costs from consumers (fine print, complex formulas)
- Steering consumers toward higher-cost products when the entity knows the consumer does not understand the difference
- Exploiting a consumer's reliance on the entity's recommendation to enrol them in products that benefit the entity at the consumer's expense
- Targeting consumer segments with demonstrably lower financial literacy or English proficiency with products whose risks those consumers cannot reasonably understand
- Using contract terms, interfaces, or processes that prevent consumers from effectively comparing product terms

## UDAAP in the Examination Process

The CFPB assesses UDAAP risk in every examination of every product line. The examination approach is:

1. **Compliance Management Review (CMR) first** — Examiners assess whether the entity has policies, procedures, training, and monitoring in place to identify and prevent UDAAPs at the firm level.

2. **Product-level UDAAP assessment** — Each product module in the examination includes a UDAAP assessment. Examiners review marketing materials, contracts, disclosures, sales scripts, call recordings, complaint data, and transaction samples.

3. **Internal consultation requirement** — Before citing a UDAAP violation, CFPB examiners must follow internal consultation requirements to confirm the applicable legal standards have been met. A UDAAP finding is not made unilaterally by the examination team.

4. **Aggregation of instances** — Individual transactions or consumer contacts may not individually constitute a UDAAP, but a pattern across the consumer population can establish unfairness (substantial injury in aggregate) or deception (widespread misleading conduct).

The facts of each case are critical. The CFPB does not apply UDAAP as a bright-line rule. Context, consumer population, disclosure quality, and the entity's intent and awareness all inform the finding.

## UDAAP and Enumerated Laws — The Relationship

UDAAP and enumerated consumer laws are complementary, not alternative:

- A practice that violates an enumerated law (e.g., a Reg Z disclosure violation) may also constitute a UDAAP if consumers were materially misled about the cost of credit.
- A practice that complies with all enumerated laws can still be a UDAAP if the conduct is unfair, deceptive, or abusive in a way not reached by the enumerated standard.
- The CFPB uses UDAAP to address emerging practices in markets where no specific enumerated law applies — this is particularly relevant for new fintech products (BNPL, digital wallets, algorithmic credit scoring, digital-native account features).

## Key Points for Agents

- **UDAAP applies to every consumer financial product in every examination.** It is not a supplementary standard — it is the CFPB's primary catch-all authority. An examination cannot be "UDAAP-free" by design. **(Dodd-Frank §1036; 12 USC 5536)**
- **"Deceptive" does not require intent.** An honest mistake that misleads consumers about material terms is deceptive if a reasonable consumer would be misled and the matter is material.
- **"Unfair" requires a net harm analysis.** The three-prong test requires both that the injury is substantial and not reasonably avoidable, AND that it is not outweighed by countervailing consumer or competitive benefits. Mere consumer dissatisfaction is not unfairness.
- **"Abusive" is unique to the CFPB and requires no financial harm.** Materially interfering with a consumer's ability to understand a product term — even without deception — is a standalone abusiveness basis. Reasonable reliance is explicitly protected. **(Dodd-Frank §1036(a)(1)(B))**
- **Omissions are as actionable as affirmative misrepresentations.** Failing to disclose material risks, costs, or conditions can establish deception without any false statement.
- **Aggregate harm matters for unfairness.** Small per-consumer financial injuries that are repeated across a large consumer population can satisfy the "substantial injury" standard in aggregate. A $3 fee charged to 5 million consumers per year is substantial.
- **UDAAP fills the gap for fintech products.** Where no specific enumerated law covers a new product feature or business model, the CFPB can and does apply UDAAP. This is its primary tool for addressing consumer harm in markets ahead of rulemaking.
- **CFPB internal consultation is required before citing a violation.** UDAAP findings go through an internal review process — examiners cannot cite a UDAAP violation without confirming the legal standard has been met through the Bureau's consultation process.
