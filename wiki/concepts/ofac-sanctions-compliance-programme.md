# OFAC — Sanctions Compliance Programme (SCP)

## Overview

OFAC's **Framework for Compliance Commitments** (May 2019) sets out what constitutes an effective Sanctions Compliance Programme (SCP). The Framework is not a standalone regulatory requirement — its significance is enforcement-facing: an adequate SCP at the time of an apparent violation is a mitigating factor under **General Factor E** (compliance programme) of the Economic Sanctions Enforcement Guidelines (Part 501 Appendix A §III.E), and corrective action taken to implement or enhance an SCP after discovery of a violation is mitigating under **General Factor F** (remedial response).

The Framework identifies five essential SCP components and, drawing from enforcement actions, ten root causes of SCP breakdowns. Financial institutions operating in sanctions-relevant environments should treat the root causes as a negative compliance checklist — each represents a pattern OFAC has previously cited as an aggravating factor.

---

## The Five Essential SCP Components

### 1 — Management Commitment

Senior management endorsement of the SCP is a prerequisite for an effective programme. Key elements:
- **Designation of a qualified OFAC compliance officer** with authority, resources, and direct access to senior management and the board
- **Adequate compliance resources** — staffing levels appropriate to the institution's risk profile; compliance is not understaffed relative to business volume
- **Culture of compliance** — tone from the top that compliance is a genuine institutional priority, not a check-the-box exercise
- Board or board committee approval of the OFAC compliance programme, policies, procedures, and controls **(Framework p. 5)**

**Enforcement relevance:** Lack of management commitment is Root Cause 4 (see below). Its presence is directly mitigating under General Factor E.

### 2 — Risk Assessment

A periodic, documented, enterprise-wide assessment of the institution's specific OFAC risk exposure. Key elements:
- Covers **customers** (including beneficial owners and counterparties), **products and services**, **geographies** (countries, jurisdictions, and currencies involved), and **transaction types**
- Identifies high-risk areas and uses the assessment to calibrate internal controls
- Updated when the institution's business, customer base, products, or the sanctions landscape materially changes
- Applies to new product development and acquisitions **(Framework pp. 6–7)**

**Enforcement relevance:** Failure to assess jurisdictional and programme-specific risks is Root Cause 6. An outdated risk assessment (Root Cause 9) leaves the programme misaligned with actual exposure.

### 3 — Internal Controls

Written policies, procedures, and controls that implement the institution's risk assessment findings. Key elements:
- **Customer and counterparty identification** — know-your-customer processes that capture sufficient information to screen against SDN List and other OFAC-maintained lists
- **Transaction screening** — systems and processes to screen transactions against applicable OFAC lists in real time or at account opening; screening must cover both customers and beneficial owners
- **Blocking and rejecting mechanics** — clear escalation paths for blocking (freeze and report) vs. rejecting (refuse and report); staff know which action applies in which circumstance
- **Escalation and notification** — clear escalation paths to compliance and senior management; defined notification procedures for blocking and rejection events
- Written policies that are **reviewed and updated** as the sanctions landscape, OFAC programmes, and the institution's risk profile change **(Framework pp. 7–8)**

**Enforcement relevance:** Inadequate or outdated internal controls (including screening tools) are Root Causes 3, 9, and 10. Absence of due diligence on customers and business partners is Root Cause 7.

### 4 — Testing and Auditing

Independent, regular testing of the SCP's effectiveness. Key elements:
- **Independent testing** — conducted by internal audit, compliance testing, or a qualified external party independent of the function being tested
- **Transaction testing** — sample-based review of transactions to verify that controls are operating as intended and that apparent violations are being identified and reported
- **Process auditing** — review of policies, procedures, screening calibration, and escalation logs
- Findings from testing are **documented, escalated, and used to improve the programme** **(Framework pp. 8–9)**

**Enforcement relevance:** Absence of independent testing means SCP breakdowns persist undetected. Failure to implement findings from testing is captured under General Factor F (remedial response).

### 5 — Training

Sanctions compliance training tailored to the institution's risk profile and to the specific roles of the personnel receiving it. Key elements:
- **Role-specific content** — front-line staff, compliance officers, relationship managers, senior management, and board members each receive training calibrated to their level and function
- **Coverage of applicable sanctions programmes** — not just general OFAC awareness but the specific programmes relevant to the institution's customers and transactions
- **Regular cadence** — training is conducted at hire and periodically thereafter; updated when sanctions programmes or internal controls materially change
- **Documented** — training completion is recorded and available for examination by regulators **(Framework pp. 9–10)**

**Enforcement relevance:** Inadequate or nonexistent training is Root Cause 2 (inadequate compliance resources/staffing) and Root Cause 1 (no formal SCP) in their training dimension.

## Ten Root Causes of SCP Breakdowns

Drawn from OFAC enforcement actions, these root causes represent the most common failures OFAC has cited:

| # | Root Cause | Corresponding SCP Component |
|---|-----------|---------------------------|
| 1 | Lack of a formal OFAC SCP | All five |
| 2 | Inadequate compliance resources or staffing | Management commitment |
| 3 | Decentralised compliance function without adequate oversight or coordination | Internal controls; management commitment |
| 4 | Lack of management commitment to the SCP | Management commitment |
| 5 | Misinterpretation or over-reliance on licensing requirements | Internal controls; training |
| 6 | Failure to identify and mitigate jurisdictional or sanctions programme risks | Risk assessment |
| 7 | Lack of due diligence on customers, business partners, or counterparties | Internal controls; risk assessment |
| 8 | Misconduct by management or compliance staff | Management commitment; testing and auditing |
| 9 | Failure to update the SCP as the sanctions landscape or business changes | Risk assessment; internal controls |
| 10 | Inadequate software, screening tools, or technical controls | Internal controls |

**(Framework pp. 8–11)**

Each root cause maps to a General Factor — primarily General Factors A (willfulness), B (awareness), and E (compliance programme) — in the penalty determination process.

## The OFAC Risk Matrix

Appendix A to Part 501 includes an OFAC Risk Matrix that financial institutions can use to evaluate their risk level. It assesses institutions as Low, Moderate, or High risk across the following dimensions:

| Dimension | Low | Moderate | High |
|-----------|-----|----------|------|
| Customer base | Stable, well-known, localised | Changing due to branching, merger, or acquisition | Large, fluctuating, international |
| High-risk customers | Few | Moderate number | Large number |
| Overseas branches / correspondent accounts | None | Some | Multiple |
| Electronic services | None or informational only | Limited e-banking | Wide array of transactional e-services |
| Funds transfers | Limited, domestic | Moderate, mostly customers | High volume, including international and non-customers |
| International transaction types | None | Limited | High number |
| OFAC action history | None; no evidence of circumstances leading to violation | Small number of recent actions (within 5 years), issues addressed | Multiple recent actions; issues not addressed |
| Management commitment | Fully assessed risk; strong commitment communicated organisation-wide | Reasonable understanding; commitment generally clear | Does not understand or has chosen to ignore OFAC compliance risk |
| Board approval | Board has approved programme with adequate policies, procedures, controls, and information systems | Board has approved programme with most key elements, some weaknesses | Board has not approved programme, or controls are significantly deficient |
| Staffing | Adequate | Generally adequate, some deficiencies | Inadequate staffing to handle workload |
| OFAC officer | Designated, authority and accountability clear | Qualified OFAC officer designated | No OFAC officer, or role unclear |
| Training | Appropriate, role-specific, covers applicable programmes, up-to-date | Adequate, some gaps | Sporadic, incomplete, or nonexistent |
| Quality control | Strong methods | Limited methods | No quality control methods |

**(Appendix A to Part 501, Annex — OFAC Risk Matrix)**

## Voluntary Self-Disclosure and the SCP

Voluntary self-disclosure (VSD) is governed by General Factor G (cooperation with OFAC) in the Enforcement Guidelines. However, the SCP interacts with VSD in two important ways:

1. **A robust SCP is what makes VSD possible.** Effective testing and auditing (SCP Component 4) detects apparent violations internally before they are discovered by OFAC or third parties — enabling voluntary self-disclosure.
2. **The SCP is independently mitigating under Factor E regardless of VSD.** Even where a violation is discovered by a third party, an institution with an adequate SCP receives mitigation under Factor E for having had the programme in place. Factor F rewards the corrective action taken after discovery.

**(Appendix A to Part 501, §III.E–G; Framework pp. 3–4)**

## Key Points for Agents

- **An adequate SCP at the time of the apparent violation directly mitigates civil penalties under General Factor E.** OFAC explicitly assesses the existence, nature, and adequacy of the institution's SCP when determining both the administrative response (cautionary letter vs. penalty) and the penalty amount. The Framework's five components define what "adequate" means in this assessment. **(Appendix A §III.E; Framework p. 4)**
- **The ten root causes are OFAC's de facto compliance checklist.** Each root cause has appeared in published enforcement actions as an aggravating factor. An institution that can demonstrate its SCP addresses all ten root causes — documented risk assessment, qualified compliance officer, independent testing, role-specific training, updated screening tools, due diligence on third parties — substantially reduces its exposure under General Factors A, B, and E. **(Framework pp. 8–11)**
- **Management commitment is the foundational SCP component.** OFAC consistently finds that SCP failures trace back to inadequate resources, unclear accountability, or absent board-level endorsement. Designating a qualified OFAC compliance officer with adequate authority, staffing, and direct board access is the structural prerequisite for the other four components to function. **(Framework p. 5)**
- **Component 4 (testing and auditing) is what enables voluntary self-disclosure.** Institutions that conduct independent transaction testing are most likely to identify apparent violations before OFAC does. Self-disclosure halves the base penalty in every quadrant of the penalty matrix and is separately mitigating under General Factor G. A programme that tests but does not self-disclose when testing reveals violations undermines both Factor E and Factor G. **(Framework pp. 8–9; Appendix A §§III.E, III.G, V.B.2)**
- **The OFAC Risk Matrix is a published self-assessment tool embedded in the Enforcement Guidelines.** Institutions can use it to identify which dimensions OFAC will assess as High risk during an examination or enforcement investigation, and to document that management has evaluated and understood the institution's risk profile — a prerequisite for Factor E mitigation. **(Appendix A, Annex)**
