# CFPB — State Law Preemption and State Enforcement

## Overview

Subtitle D (§§1041–1048) addresses the relationship between federal consumer financial law and state law. Title X deliberately departs from a federal-supremacy preemption model. Rather than providing that federal consumer financial law displaces state consumer financial laws, Subtitle D expressly preserves state law that affords consumers *greater* protections than federal floors. States function as laboratories for consumer financial protection; the federal floor does not become the federal ceiling.

Subtitle D also grants state AGs an independent right to enforce federal consumer financial law, establishes a framework for coordinating federal and state enforcement, and preserves the existing OCC preemption framework that governs national banks — a framework that Title X left substantially intact.

---

## General Preservation of State Consumer Financial Laws (§1041)

### The Rule

Federal consumer financial law does **not** preempt any provision of state law applicable to the offering or provision of a consumer financial product or service that:
1. Provides greater consumer protection than the federal standard; or
2. Is not otherwise inconsistent with federal consumer financial law **(§1041(a))**

### What "Inconsistent" Means

A state law is *inconsistent* with federal consumer financial law — and therefore preempted to the extent of the inconsistency — only where the state law:
- **Prevents or significantly interferes** with the exercise of authority conferred by federal consumer financial law; **or**
- **Requires a covered person** to violate federal consumer financial law in order to comply with state law **(§1041(a))**

**Critically, a state law is NOT inconsistent merely because it imposes additional requirements beyond federal minimums.** A state disclosure requirement that demands more information than Reg Z requires, or a state usury limit stricter than no federal usury cap, is not preempted. The federal floor is genuinely a floor.

### Practical Consequences

- A California consumer financial statute that requires additional waiting periods before signing, or a New York statute that caps fees below federal thresholds, survives preemption analysis under §1041
- A fintech operating across multiple states must comply with the strictest applicable requirement in each state — there is no federal preemption safety net
- State consumer protection acts (UDAP statutes) remain in force alongside CFPB UDAAP authority

## State Attorney General Enforcement of Federal Consumer Financial Law (§1042)

### Enforcement Right

The attorney general (or equivalent) of any State may bring a civil action in any appropriate US district court — or in the courts of that State — to enforce provisions of Title X or regulations thereunder, and to secure relief including:
- Enjoining the conduct that constitutes the violation
- Enforcing compliance with Title X
- Obtaining damages, restitution, or other compensation on behalf of state residents
- Obtaining civil penalties under the three-tier structure of §1055 **(§1042(a))**

### Prior Notice to the Bureau

Before filing a civil action under §1042, the state AG must provide **prior written notice** to the Bureau — except where providing prior notice is not practicable, in which case the AG must provide notice as soon as practicable after filing. **(§1042(b)(1))**

The Bureau is entitled to a copy of the complaint and all papers relating to the action. **(§1042(b)(2))**

### Bureau Intervention

The Bureau may **intervene** in any state AG action brought under §1042 and be heard as a party to the action. The Bureau may remove the case to federal district court if it was brought in state court. **(§1042(b)(3))**

### Limitation: Concurrent Federal Action

If the Bureau has already instituted its own enforcement proceedings against a covered person for a particular violation, **no State supervisory agency** may bring or maintain a civil action for a violation of the same conduct that is the subject of the Bureau's pending proceeding, against any defendant named in the Bureau's complaint. **(§1042(b)(4))**

This coordination rule prevents duplicative federal and state enforcement actions against the same defendant for the same conduct.

### Scope of State AG Authority

State AGs may bring actions against **non-bank covered persons** under §1042. State enforcement authority with respect to **national banks** and **federal savings associations** is governed by their own respective preemption frameworks (§§1044–1045) and does not expand by virtue of §1042.

---

## Savings Provisions (§1043)

Title X does not limit or restrict the authority of a State official or agency to administer and enforce the laws of that State, including its consumer financial protection statutes. State authority is additive — it runs alongside federal authority, not in subordination to it (except where specific preemption applies). **(§1043)**

## National Bank Preemption — No Change from Pre-Dodd-Frank Framework (§1044)

### Title X Did Not Alter National Bank Act Preemption

The preemption framework for national banks is governed by the National Bank Act (12 USC 1 et seq.) and is administered by the OCC — not the CFPB. Title X explicitly preserved this framework and did not transfer preemption authority over national banks to the Bureau. **(§1044(a))**

A state consumer financial law is preempted with respect to a national bank only if:
1. The OCC determines, in accordance with Barnett Bank and on a case-by-case basis supported by substantial evidence, that the state law **prevents or significantly interferes** with the exercise of national bank powers; or
2. The state law is otherwise preempted by federal law applicable to national banks (e.g., the National Bank Act, Federal Deposit Insurance Act, or another federal statute)

### The Barnett Bank Standard

The *Barnett Bank of Marion County, N.A. v. Nelson* (1996) standard — not OCC's prior "obstacle" preemption interpretations — governs whether a state law is preempted with respect to national banks. Under *Barnett Bank*, a state law is preempted only if it *prevents or significantly interferes* with the exercise of a national bank's powers. State laws that merely affect or regulate how a bank exercises its powers are not necessarily preempted. **(§1044(a))**

### De Novo Judicial Review of OCC Preemption Determinations (§1047)

OCC determinations that a state law is preempted as applied to national banks are subject to de novo judicial review in federal court — not the deferential review that applies to agency interpretations of their own statutes. Courts must make independent legal judgments about preemption, without giving Chevron deference to OCC's legal conclusions. **(§1047)**

This is a significant constraint on the scope of OCC preemption authority: OCC cannot expand national bank preemption through administrative interpretation without federal court endorsement under de novo review.

### No CFPB Preemption Authority Over National Banks

The CFPB does not have authority to preempt state consumer financial laws as applied to national banks. Preemption for national banks flows from the National Bank Act and OCC determinations, not from Title X. **(§1044)**

---

## Federal Savings Association Preemption (§1045)

Federal savings associations (FSAs) supervised by OCC (post-OTS abolition) are subject to the same preemption framework as national banks under §1045 — state consumer financial laws are preempted only if they prevent or significantly interfere with FSA powers, determined on a case-by-case basis by OCC with de novo judicial review. The CFPB does not have authority to preempt state consumer financial laws as applied to FSAs. **(§1045)**

---

## Visitorial Powers Over National Banks (§1046)

### What Visitorial Powers Are

Visitorial powers are the rights of regulators to examine, inspect, and supervise the operations of supervised entities. For national banks, visitorial powers are traditionally reserved to the OCC — they are not shared with other federal or state regulators.

### Title X Preserved OCC's Exclusive Visitorial Powers

Title X did not grant the Bureau visitorial powers over national banks. The OCC retains exclusive visitorial powers over national banks under the National Bank Act. **(§1046)**

The Bureau exercises its supervisory authority over large national banks (those with assets above $10 billion) through coordinated examination processes with the OCC rather than through independent Bureau visits. In practice, the Bureau and OCC conduct joint or parallel examinations.

### State Visitorial Powers

State regulators continue to lack visitorial powers over national banks under the National Bank Act. Title X did not change this. State attorneys general may enforce federal consumer financial law under §1042, but they cannot examine national banks. **(§1046)**

## Preemption Interaction with Enumerated Consumer Laws

For non-bank covered persons, the enumerated consumer laws (ECOA, EFTA, FCRA, TILA, etc.) each contain their own preemption provisions, which govern whether their specific subject matter preempts state law. Title X's general preemption framework in §1041 operates as a default for matters not covered by enumerated-law-specific preemption provisions.

Key examples:
- **TILA/Reg Z:** Contains specific preemption provisions for disclosure content — state disclosure requirements inconsistent with TILA disclosure requirements are preempted; state laws providing additional protections are generally not preempted
- **FCRA:** Contains specific preemption provisions for CRA obligations — state laws on credit reporting are largely preempted by FCRA for certain subject matters
- **ECOA/Reg B:** State credit discrimination laws that provide greater protections to applicants coexist with ECOA

Where an enumerated consumer law contains specific preemption provisions, those provisions govern rather than the general §1041 framework.

## Key Points for Agents

- **Title X is a floor, not a ceiling.** Federal consumer financial law does not preempt state laws that provide greater consumer protection. A fintech operating nationally must map state requirements in addition to federal requirements in every jurisdiction where it serves consumers. **(§1041)**
- **State AGs have independent enforcement authority over federal consumer financial law for non-bank covered persons.** A fintech can face enforcement action from the CFPB and simultaneously (or sequentially) from state AGs in states where its consumers are harmed — unless the CFPB has already filed proceedings against the same defendant for the same conduct. **(§1042)**
- **The CFPB has no preemption authority over national banks.** National bank preemption runs through the OCC under the National Bank Act and *Barnett Bank* standard. A bank that wants to preempt a state consumer financial requirement must seek OCC preemption determination, not CFPB determination. **(§1044)**
- **OCC preemption determinations are subject to de novo judicial review** — courts do not defer to OCC's legal conclusions about whether a state law is preempted. This makes preemption harder to obtain and easier to challenge judicially than under a deference regime. **(§1047)**
- **State enforcement and CFPB enforcement are concurrent, not exclusive.** State AGs enforcing CFPB-administered statutes against non-bank covered persons must give notice to the Bureau, and the Bureau may intervene — but the state's right to bring the action is not conditioned on the Bureau's approval. **(§1042(b))**
