# CFPB — Enforcement Powers and Civil Penalty Framework

## Overview

Subtitle E (§§1051–1057) grants the Bureau a comprehensive civil enforcement toolkit. The Bureau has **no criminal enforcement authority** — it cannot bring criminal prosecutions, indict, or impose criminal penalties. Its arsenal consists of administrative and judicial civil remedies: civil investigative demands to gather information, administrative cease-and-desist proceedings, civil lawsuits in federal district court, and a three-tier civil monetary penalty structure with a maximum of $1,000,000 per day for knowing violations. Subtitle E also establishes whistleblower protections for employees of covered persons who report potential violations.

---

## Civil Investigative Demands (§1052)

### Authority

Before initiating a formal enforcement proceeding, the Bureau may issue civil investigative demands (CIDs) to **any person** believed to have information relevant to a violation of federal consumer financial law that the Bureau is investigating. CIDs are not limited to covered persons — they may be issued to third parties, vendors, or former employees. **(§1052(a))**

### What a CID May Require

A CID may require the recipient to produce:
- **Documentary evidence** — any written or recorded material
- **Tangible things** — physical items relevant to the investigation
- **Written reports or answers to interrogatories** — written responses to specified questions
- **Oral testimony** — deposition-style testimony under oath **(§1052(a)(1))**

### CID Requirements

A CID must:
1. State the **nature of the conduct** under investigation and the provision of law applicable to such conduct
2. Identify the **documents, tangible things, written reports, or testimony** sought
3. Contain instructions for **producing the materials** and provide a reasonable compliance deadline

A CID may not require the production of documentary evidence or tangible things that would be protected by attorney-client privilege, attorney work-product doctrine, or any other applicable legal privilege. **(§1052(d))**

### Petition to Modify or Set Aside

Before the compliance deadline, a recipient may petition the Director to modify or set aside the CID. The Director must rule on the petition before the compliance deadline. **(§1052(f))**

### Enforcement in Federal Court

If a person fails to comply with a CID, the Bureau may bring an action in any federal district court with personal jurisdiction over the person. The court may issue orders requiring compliance. **(§1052(e))**

## Administrative Cease-and-Desist Proceedings (§§1053–1054)

### Notice of Charges

The Bureau may initiate administrative enforcement proceedings by issuing a notice of charges against a covered person or service provider. The notice must state the legal basis for the proceeding and the conduct alleged to constitute a violation. The proceeding is heard by an administrative law judge (ALJ) within the Bureau. **(§1053(b))**

### Temporary Orders (§1053(c))

Pending the outcome of administrative proceedings, the Bureau may issue a **temporary cease-and-desist order** without a hearing if:
- The Bureau has initiated administrative proceedings; **and**
- The Bureau reasonably determines that the conduct alleged poses an imminent risk of harm to consumers

Temporary orders are effective **immediately upon service** and remain in effect for up to **60 days** unless the respondent consents to a longer period or the administrative proceedings conclude first. **(§1053(c))**

### Final Orders

Upon completion of administrative proceedings, the Bureau may issue final orders that may require:
- **Cessation of the violating conduct**
- **Affirmative action** to correct the condition resulting from the violation
- **Restitution** to consumers who suffered harm
- **Disgorgement** of unjust enrichment
- **Civil monetary penalties** (see tier table below)
- **Prohibition** from participation in consumer financial services industries (permanent ban) **(§1053(e))**

### Consent Orders

The Bureau and a respondent may enter a consent order at any stage of proceedings. Consent orders become final when accepted by the Bureau and are binding on the respondent to the same extent as a final order after full proceedings.

## Civil Actions in Federal Court (§1054)

In addition to administrative proceedings, the Bureau may bring civil actions directly in the United States District Court for the District of Columbia or any district where the defendant resides or does business. The court has authority to grant:
- **Injunctive and other equitable relief** — including permanent injunctions prohibiting continuation of the conduct
- **Rescission or reformation** of consumer contracts entered into in violation of federal consumer financial law
- **Restitution** to harmed consumers
- **Disgorgement** of gains obtained through the violation
- **Civil monetary penalties** under the three-tier structure (see below)
- **Public notification** of the violation through publication in the Federal Register

### Statute of Limitations

A civil action must be brought **within 3 years** after the Bureau discovers the violation, or **within 5 years** after the date the violation occurs — whichever is earlier. **(§1054(a))**

## Civil Monetary Penalty Tiers (§1055)

The Bureau's civil monetary penalty structure contains three tiers, each calibrated to the culpability of the violator:

| Tier | Maximum per day | Standard |
|------|----------------|----------|
| **Tier 1** | $5,000 | Any violation of federal consumer financial law (no culpability qualifier) |
| **Tier 2** | $25,000 | Reckless engagement in a violation of federal consumer financial law |
| **Tier 3** | $1,000,000 | Knowing violation of federal consumer financial law |

### How Penalties Accumulate

- Penalties accrue **per day** for each day on which the violation continues
- A multi-month enforcement period can result in penalties of tens or hundreds of millions of dollars at Tier 3
- The maximum per-day amounts are subject to annual inflation adjustment (the CFPB periodically publishes updated maximums in the Federal Register)
- Separate violations or separate consumers affected may result in separate penalty calculations

### Factors Considered in Penalty Assessment

Neither the statute nor Bureau guidance specifies mandatory factors, but the Bureau's practice and published consent orders reflect the following considerations:
- Degree of harm to consumers (number of consumers, dollar amounts, nature of harm)
- Whether the respondent cooperated with the investigation
- Prior supervisory history (prior MRAs or enforcement actions)
- Whether remediation was voluntary or compelled
- Duration of the violation
- Culpability level (Tier 1/2/3 classification)
- Respondent's financial resources (ability to pay)

### Penalty Proceeds

Civil monetary penalties collected by the Bureau are deposited in the **Consumer Financial Civil Penalty Fund**, administered by the Bureau. The Bureau may use Fund proceeds to pay consumer redress, education, and financial literacy programs. Fund proceeds are not returned to the US Treasury. **(§1017(d))**

## Remedies Beyond Penalties (§1055)

In any enforcement action (administrative or judicial), the Bureau may obtain:

### Restitution
Return to consumers of money or property they paid or lost as a result of the violation. Restitution is backward-looking — it compensates for past harm. In major enforcement actions restitution awards commonly dwarf associated civil penalties.

### Disgorgement
Forfeiture of net profits obtained through the violation. Disgorgement is aimed at preventing unjust enrichment, not compensating consumers. The Bureau may seek both restitution and disgorgement for the same conduct.

### Rescission and Reformation
Cancellation of consumer contracts entered into in violation of federal consumer financial law, and court-ordered amendment of contract terms to eliminate illegal provisions. These remedies are particularly relevant in cases involving disclosure violations (TILA, RESPA) or prohibited credit terms.

### Activity Restrictions
Restrictions on the types of consumer financial products or services the respondent may offer, or prohibition from participating in the consumer financial services industry. These "conduct remedies" are imposed in addition to monetary relief in cases involving systemic violations.

---

## Relationship Between Administrative and Judicial Proceedings

The Bureau may pursue enforcement through administrative proceedings, federal court, or both. In practice:
- **Administrative proceedings** are more commonly used for covered entities with ongoing supervisory relationships — they allow the Bureau to resolve violations within a structured regulatory process
- **Federal court actions** are used where the Bureau seeks injunctive relief, where the respondent is not a covered entity (e.g., an individual), or where the Bureau wants to establish public precedent through a court judgment

A final administrative order has the same legal force as a federal court judgment for purposes of enforcement.

## Whistleblower Protection (§1057)

### Protected Activities

Employees and former employees of covered persons are protected against retaliation for any of the following activities:
1. Providing information to the Bureau or any other State or Federal regulatory or law enforcement agency about a possible violation of federal consumer financial law
2. Participating in, initiating, or assisting in any investigation, examination, or proceeding of the Bureau or a State or Federal agency regarding such a violation
3. Filing a complaint, providing information, or assisting in any proceeding about a possible violation of federal consumer financial law
4. Objecting to or refusing to participate in conduct the employee reasonably believed to be in violation of federal consumer financial law
5. Providing information to the employer's internal compliance or legal department regarding a potential violation **(§1057(a))**

### Prohibited Retaliation

No covered person may discharge, demote, suspend, threaten, harass, or in any other manner discriminate against an employee because of any protected activity. **(§1057(a))**

### Complaint Process

A whistleblower who suffers retaliation must file a complaint with the **Secretary of Labor** within **180 days** of the date the retaliatory act occurred. **(§1057(b)(1))**

The complaint triggers an investigation by the Department of Labor. If the Labor Secretary finds reasonable cause, the matter proceeds to a hearing. The employee bears the initial burden of demonstrating that protected activity was a contributing factor in the adverse action. The burden then shifts to the employer to demonstrate by clear and convincing evidence that the adverse action would have occurred even absent the protected activity.

### Remedies for Retaliation

If retaliation is established, remedies include:
- **Reinstatement** to the same seniority level the employee would have held absent the retaliation
- **Back pay with interest** for the period of discrimination
- **Compensation for litigation costs, expert witness fees, and reasonable attorneys' fees** **(§1057(c))**

### No Confidentiality Agreements

Covered persons may not require employees to sign confidentiality agreements that would prohibit or restrict employees from reporting potential violations to the Bureau or other regulators. Such agreements are void as contrary to public policy.

## State Attorney General Enforcement Coordination (§1042)

Although §1042 is in Subtitle D, it directly intersects with the Subtitle E enforcement framework:
- State AGs may bring civil actions on behalf of their residents to enforce federal consumer financial law against non-bank covered persons
- State AGs must notify the Bureau before filing (or immediately after in exigent circumstances)
- The Bureau may intervene in state AG actions and be heard as a party
- If the Bureau has already initiated enforcement proceedings, state agencies may not bring concurrent proceedings against the same defendant for the same conduct **(§1042)**

## Key Points for Agents

- **The Bureau has no criminal enforcement power.** All Bureau enforcement is civil. Criminal referrals to DOJ or state prosecutors occur through coordination, not through the Bureau's own enforcement authority. A Bureau enforcement action cannot result in imprisonment. **(§1054)**
- **The three-tier penalty structure ($5K/$25K/$1M per day) turns on culpability.** "Knowing" is the highest tier and produces the largest penalties. A covered person that continues a practice after a Bureau examination identifies it as problematic faces Tier 3 exposure. **(§1055)**
- **CIDs precede formal enforcement and are not limited to covered persons.** Any third party with relevant information can receive a CID. A fintech's vendors, investors, or technology partners may be CID recipients in an investigation of the fintech. **(§1052)**
- **Restitution in Bureau actions routinely exceeds civil penalties.** The civil penalty fund and the restitution obligation are separate. In large consumer redress cases, the restitution pool (returned to consumers) is often an order of magnitude larger than the associated civil penalty. **(§1055)**
- **Whistleblower protection covers internal reporting, not just Bureau reporting.** An employee who reports a concern to the company's own compliance team is protected from retaliation. Terminating an employee for raising internal compliance concerns is a §1057 violation even if the employee never contacted the Bureau. **(§1057(a)(5))**
