# FCA Financial Crime Guide — FCG 2: Financial Crime Systems and Controls

## What This Chapter Is

FCG 2 is the foundational governance chapter of the FCA's Financial Crime Guide. It sets out the FCA's expectations for the overarching systems and controls that firms must have in place to defend against all types of financial crime — AML, fraud, sanctions, bribery and corruption, data security — before any specific financial crime type is addressed in later chapters (FCG 3–FCG 12).

The chapter provides seven themes, each with self-assessment questions and good/poor practice tables. It is guidance, not rules — but the FCA uses it as a benchmark in supervisory assessments and enforcement.

**Legal anchor:** SYSC 6.1.1R (compliance policies and financial crime systems obligation — common platform firms) and SYSC 3.2.6R (equivalent for non-common-platform firms). See [[sysc6-compliance-internal-audit-financial-crime]].

---

## Scope (FCG 2.1)

**Who must read this chapter:**
- All firms subject to SYSC 3.2.6R or SYSC 6.1.1R
- EMIs and PIs within FCA supervisory scope
- Annex I financial institutions subject to the Money Laundering Regulations (not subject to SYSC financial crime rules, but the guidance assists MLR compliance) **(FCG 2.1.2)**

All firms must take steps to defend themselves against financial crime. A variety of approaches is possible — the chapter provides themes, not a single prescribed structure. **(FCG 2.1.3)**

---

## FCG 2.2 — Seven Governance Themes

### 1. Governance (FCG 2.2.1)

Senior management must take **clear responsibility** for managing financial crime risks, treating them in the same manner as other business risks. There must be evidence that senior management are **actively engaged** in the firm's approach.

**Self-assessment questions:**
- When did senior management (including the board or appropriate sub-committees) last consider financial crime issues? What action followed?
- How are senior management kept up to date on financial crime issues?
- Is there evidence that issues have been **escalated** where warranted?

| Good practice | Poor practice |
|---|---|
| Senior management set the right tone and demonstrate leadership on financial crime | Little evidence of senior staff involvement and challenge in practice |
| Firm takes active steps to prevent criminals taking advantage of its services | Firm concentrates on narrow compliance with minimum regulatory standards |
| FCA draws comfort from evidence that these practices take place | Financial crime issues dealt with on a purely reactive basis |
| Firm has a strategy for self-improvement on financial crime | No meaningful record or evidence of senior management considering financial crime risks |
| Clear criteria for escalating financial crime issues | — |

Firms should consider their SM&CR arrangements when reviewing senior management responsibility for financial crime. **(FCG 2.2.1)**

---

### 2. Management Information (FCG 2.2.2)

MI should give senior management **sufficient information** to understand the financial crime risks to which the firm is exposed and to adhere to the firm's own risk appetite. MI should be provided regularly and ad hoc as risk dictates.

**Five categories of financial crime MI:**
1. Overview of financial crime risks the firm faces — including emerging risks and changes to the risk assessment
2. Legal and regulatory developments and their impact on the firm's approach
3. Overview of the effectiveness of the firm's financial crime systems and controls
4. Overview of staff expenses, gifts, hospitality, and charitable donations — including rejected claims
5. Information about individual business relationships — specifically:
   - Number and nature of **new** business relationships (particularly high-risk ones)
   - Number and nature of relationships **terminated** for financial crime concerns
   - Number of **transaction monitoring alerts**
   - Details of any **true sanction hits**
   - Information about **SARs** considered or submitted

MI may come from multiple sources: compliance department, internal audit, MLRO, or nominated officer. **(FCG 2.2.2)**

---

### 3. Structure (FCG 2.2.3)

Firms' organisational structures may differ. There is no one "right answer" — the structure should promote **coordination** and **information sharing** across the business.

**Self-assessment questions:**
- Who has ultimate responsibility for: (a) AML; (b) fraud prevention; (c) data security; (d) CTF; (e) anti-bribery and corruption; (f) financial sanctions?
- Do staff have appropriate seniority and experience, along with clear reporting lines?
- Does the structure promote a coordinated approach and accountability?
- Are the firm's financial crime teams **adequately resourced**? Are budgets proportionate to the risks?
- In smaller firms: do those with financial crime responsibilities have other roles? (Dual roles are acceptable but consider whether staff are spread too thinly and whether conflicts of interest arise.)

| Good practice | Poor practice |
|---|---|
| Financial crime risks addressed in a coordinated manner across the business; information shared readily | Firm makes no effort to understand or address gaps in its financial crime defences |
| Management responsible for financial crime are sufficiently senior, credible, independent, and experienced | Financial crime officers relatively junior; often overruled without documented justification |
| Firm has considered how counter-fraud and AML efforts can complement each other | Financial crime departments under-resourced; senior management reluctant to address |
| Firm bolsters insufficient in-house knowledge with external expertise | — |

**(FCG 2.2.3)**

---

### 4. Risk Assessment (FCG 2.2.4)

A thorough understanding of **financial crime risks** is key to applying proportionate and effective systems and controls.

**Business-wide risk assessment (BWRA)** should:
- Be **comprehensive** and consider a wide range of factors — it is not normally enough to consider just one factor
- Draw on **a wide range of relevant information** — it is not normally enough to use just one source
- Be **proportionate** to the nature, scale, and complexity of the firm's activities

**Risk factors to consider:** products and services offered; jurisdictions operated in; types of customer attracted; complexity and volume of transactions; distribution channels used.

**Individual relationship risk assessment** builds on the BWRA to determine the risk associated with specific business relationships. It is not a substitute for the BWRA.

Both BWRA and individual risk assessments should be **regularly reviewed** to remain current.

**Self-assessment questions:**
- What are the main financial crime risks to the business?
- How does the firm seek to understand the risks it faces?
- When did the firm last update its risk assessment?
- How do you identify new or emerging financial crime risks?
- Is risk considered and recorded systematically, with assessments updated and signed off?
- Who challenges risk assessments — and is this process sufficiently rigorous and well-documented?
- How do procedures adapt to emerging risks?

| Good practice | Poor practice |
|---|---|
| Risk assessment is comprehensive | Risk assessment is a one-off exercise |
| Continuous process based on best information from internal and external sources | Efforts to understand risk are piecemeal and lack coordination |
| Concentrates resources where risks are greatest | Risk assessments are incomplete |
| Considers the **impact of crime on customers** (not just fraud against the firm) | Targets financial crimes that affect the bottom line but neglects those where third parties suffer (e.g. fraud against customers) |
| Considers financial crime risk when **designing new products and services** | — |

**(FCG 2.2.4)**

---

### 5. Policies and Procedures (FCG 2.2.5)

A firm must have **up-to-date** policies and procedures appropriate to its business. These should be **readily accessible, effective, and understood** by all relevant staff.

**Self-assessment questions:**
- How often are policies and procedures reviewed, and at what level of seniority?
- How does the policy framework mitigate the financial crime risks identified?
- What steps does the firm take to ensure policies reflect new risks or external events quickly?
- How does the firm ensure staff understand its policies?
- For larger groups: how are policies and procedures disseminated and applied throughout the business?

| Good practice | Poor practice |
|---|---|
| Clear documentation of the firm's approach to its legal and regulatory requirements on financial crime | No written policies and procedures |
| Policies and procedures regularly reviewed and updated | Firm does not tailor externally produced policies to suit its business |
| Internal audit or another independent party monitors the effectiveness of policies, procedures, systems and controls | Firm fails to review policies in light of events |
| — | Firm fails to check whether policies are applied consistently and effectively |
| — | Firm has not considered whether policies are consistent with obligations under legislation that forbids discrimination |

See SYSC 3.2.6R and SYSC 6.1.1R. **(FCG 2.2.5)**

---

### 6. Staff Recruitment, Vetting, Training, Awareness and Remuneration (FCG 2.2.6)

Firms must employ staff with the skills, knowledge and expertise to carry out their functions effectively. Firms should also manage the risk of staff being **rewarded for taking unacceptable financial crime risks** (SYSC 19A.3.51R — Remuneration Principle 12(h), for firms subject to the Remuneration Code).

**Self-assessment questions:**
- How are staff vetted? Does vetting reflect the financial crime risks staff are exposed to?
- How does the firm ensure staff are aware of financial crime risks and their obligations?
- Do staff have access to training on an appropriate range of financial crime risks?
- How does the firm ensure training is of consistent quality and kept up to date?
- Is training tailored to particular roles?
- How is the effectiveness of training assessed?
- When was training material last reviewed?

| Good practice | Poor practice |
|---|---|
| Staff in higher-risk roles subject to more thorough vetting | Staff not competent to carry out preventative functions, exposing firm to financial crime risk |
| Temporary staff in higher-risk roles vetted to the same standard as permanent staff | Staff vetting is a one-off exercise |
| Where employment agencies are used, firm periodically satisfies itself the agency adheres to agreed vetting standards | Firm fails to identify changes that could affect an individual's integrity and suitability |
| Tailored training to ensure staff knowledge is adequate and up to date | Firm limits enhanced vetting to senior management; fails to vet staff whose roles expose them to higher FC risk |
| New staff in customer-facing positions receive FC training tailored to their role before interacting with customers | Firm fails to identify whether staff exposed to bribery/corruption risk have links to relevant political/administrative decision-makers |
| Training has a strong practical dimension (case studies) and some form of testing | Poor compliance records not reflected in staff appraisals and remuneration |
| Firm satisfies itself that staff understand their responsibilities (e.g. computerised training includes a test) | Training dwells unduly on legislation and regulations rather than practical examples |
| Whistleblowing procedures are clear and accessible, and respect staff confidentiality | Training material not kept up to date |
| — | Firm fails to identify training needs |
| — | No training logs or tracking of employees' training history |
| — | Training content lacks management sign-off |
| — | Training does not cover whistleblowing and escalation procedures |

See SYSC 3.1.6R and SYSC 5.1.1R. **(FCG 2.2.6)**

---

### 7. Quality of Oversight (FCG 2.2.7)

A firm's efforts to combat financial crime should be subject to **challenge**. Senior management must ensure that policies and procedures are appropriate and followed.

**Self-assessment questions:**
- How does the firm ensure its approach to reviewing the effectiveness of financial crime systems controls is **comprehensive**?
- What are the findings of recent internal audits and compliance reviews on financial crime topics?
- How has the firm progressed remedial measures?

| Good practice | Poor practice |
|---|---|
| Internal audit and compliance routinely test the firm's defences against financial crime, including specific financial crime threats | Compliance unit and audit teams lack experience in financial crime matters |
| Decisions on allocation of compliance and audit resource are **risk-based** | Compliance conclusions not shared between business units; lessons not spread more widely |
| Management engages constructively with processes of oversight and challenge | — |
| Smaller firms seek external help if needed | — |

**(FCG 2.2.7)**

## Relationship to Other FCG Chapters

FCG 2 is the governance baseline. Later chapters apply these themes to specific financial crime types:
- **FCG 3** — Money laundering and terrorist financing (see [[fcg3-money-laundering-terrorist-financing]])
- **FCG 7** — Sanctions, asset freezes and proliferation financing (see [[fcg7-sanctions-asset-freezes-proliferation-financing]])

The seven FCG 2 themes recur in each subsequent chapter, adapted to the specific risks of that financial crime type. A firm with poor FCG 2 foundations — weak governance, inadequate risk assessment, untailored policies — will typically have deficiencies across all the specific chapters too.

---

## Key Points for Agents

- FCG 2 applies to all firms subject to SYSC 3.2.6R or SYSC 6.1.1R, plus EMIs and PIs — it is the universal governance baseline for all financial crime compliance, not just AML.
- Senior management must take clear, evidenced responsibility for financial crime. The FCA specifically looks for evidence of active engagement, challenge, and escalation — not just delegation. **(FCG 2.2.1)**
- The MI requirements include transaction monitoring alert volumes, true sanction hits, and SARs submitted — these are specific metrics the FCA expects to see reported to senior management. **(FCG 2.2.2)**
- The business-wide risk assessment must be comprehensive, multi-source, and regularly reviewed. A one-off exercise is explicitly identified as poor practice. It must consider the impact of crime on customers — not only losses to the firm. **(FCG 2.2.4)**
- Training must be practical (case studies, testing), tailored to role, and tracked (training logs). No training logs, management sign-off failures, and training content that dwells only on legislation are all poor practice. **(FCG 2.2.6)**
- Staff remuneration must not reward unacceptable financial crime risks — Remuneration Principle 12(h) (SYSC 19A.3.51R) applies to Remuneration Code firms. **(FCG 2.2.6)**
